# Zinc+: SNARKs over Polynomial Rings, or How to Stop Paying for XOR

*A hash-based proof system that arithmetizes SHA-256 in 19 trace columns by letting constraints live in Q[X] and F_q[X] with ideal-membership predicates, plus a new MDS code family that makes Brakedown-style IOPPs work over the rationals.*

**Paper:** https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/855.pdf

## TL;DR

Every general-purpose SNARK in production speaks one language: polynomial constraints over a single finite field F_q. Operations that don't translate cleanly into that language — XOR, bit rotation, mod-2³² arithmetic, lattice-ring operations — get "arithmetized" into the field, which inflates witnesses by one to two orders of magnitude. Zinc+ replaces the single-field constraint system with a Universal Constraint System (UCS) that lets you write algebraic *and* ideal-membership constraints simultaneously over Q[X], Z[X], and any number of F_q[X], and compiles those constraints down through any existing finite-field PIOP. The other half of the paper is Zip+, a Brakedown-style IOPP for polynomial-ring witnesses built on a new code family — Integer Pseudo-Reed-Solomon — that gives Reed-Solomon-style FFT encoding over Q without exponential coefficient blowup. The reference implementation proves 7 SHA-256 compressions plus the multi-scalar-multiplication part of ECDSA in 40.6 ms / 7.0 ms / 198 KB on a MacBook Air M4, at 100 bits of security, unoptimized, with a trace of 29 rows and 19 witness columns.

## Math you'll need

### Polynomial rings and ideals

A polynomial ring R[X] is what you get by taking polynomials in one variable X with coefficients drawn from a ring R. The paper cares about three flavors: F_q[X] (the familiar one, polynomials over a prime field), Z[X] (integer coefficients, no modular reduction available), and Q[X] (rational coefficients). An **ideal** generated by a polynomial g(X), written (g), is the set of all R[X]-multiples of g. Saying f ∈ (g) is the same as saying g divides f, which is the same as saying f vanishes in the quotient ring R[X]/(g).

Two ideals carry almost all the weight in this paper. The ideal (X − 2) over Q[X] is the kernel of the homomorphism "substitute X = 2": a polynomial is in (X − 2) exactly when it evaluates to zero at X = 2. The ideal (X^w − 1) is the kernel of "reduce mod X^w − 1", which for binary-coefficient polynomials acts like a cyclic shift. Both turn into clean algebraic claims once you stop thinking of R[X] as "polynomials" and start thinking of it as a ring with these extra structural operations available cheap.

If finite fields were the only setting you'd ever seen, ideals would feel exotic. The translation: a finite field F_q is itself the quotient Z/(q); you "live in" the residues modulo q. Ideals over R[X] are exactly the same idea, generalized — pick a polynomial g, decide that everything congruent mod g is the same thing, and you have a new arithmetic with new identities to exploit. For a worked example: in Q[X]/(X² + 1), the element X behaves exactly like the imaginary unit i, because X² = −1 in the quotient.

### Bitstrings as polynomials, with rotation and XOR for free

A 32-bit word is a sequence (b₀, b₁, …, b₃₁) of zeros and ones. Treat it instead as the polynomial a(X) = b₀ + b₁·X + b₂·X² + … + b₃₁·X³¹, an element of {0,1}^{<32}[X] ⊆ Q[X]. Evaluating at X = 2 gives the integer the bitstring represents. Critically, the set {0,1}^{<32}[X] is *not* a subring of F₂[X] — over Q the polynomial 1 + X plus itself is 2 + 2X, not 0 — which is exactly what makes both addition-as-integer-arithmetic and XOR-as-mod-2-arithmetic accessible from the same representation.

Three identities form the working vocabulary. Rotation left by one bit: b(X) − X·a(X) ∈ (X³² − 1) forces b to be the cyclic shift of a. Bitstring-to-integer: a(X) − c ∈ (X − 2) with a ∈ {0,1}^{<32}[X] and c ∈ [0, 2³² − 1] forces c to be the integer represented by a's bits. XOR: project a and b into F₂[X] by reducing coefficients mod 2 (call that map φ₂); then a XOR b equals φ₂(a) + φ₂(b) — one polynomial addition in F₂[X], no per-bit constraints.

The point of these encodings isn't conceptual elegance. It's that one constraint replaces what would otherwise be 32 boolean constraints plus a stack of decomposition columns. SHA-256's Σ₀ function, defined as ROTR²(x) XOR ROTR¹³(x) XOR ROTR²²(x), becomes a single ideal-membership claim instead of an entire subcircuit.

### Multilinear extensions and ring projections

The multilinear extension (MLE) of a vector v ∈ R^{2^μ} indexed by μ-bit strings is the unique multilinear polynomial v̂ in μ variables that agrees with v on {0,1}^μ. The whole sumcheck/PIOP machinery rests on one observation: if v has any nonzero entry, then v̂ evaluated at a uniformly random r ∈ F^μ is nonzero except with probability μ/|F|. So "this vector is all zeros" becomes "this polynomial vanishes at a random point", which is a single field element to send. The standard notation is v̂(r) = Σ_b ẽq(b, r) · v_b, with ẽq the multilinear equality indicator.

Zinc+ adds a second operation on top of MLE: **ring projection**. The maps that appear in the paper are φ_q (reduce the coefficients of a polynomial in Z[X] modulo a prime q, landing in F_q[X]) and ψ_a (evaluate a polynomial in F_q[X] at a fixed element a ∈ F_q, landing in F_q). The verifier samples these projections at random, and the polynomial-ring constraint collapses, one ring at a time, into a plain finite-field constraint.

The combinatorics matter. A Q[X]-constraint becomes an F_q̃[X]-constraint when you sample a random Ω(λ)-bit prime q₀ (λ being the security parameter, think 128). That F_q̃[X]-constraint becomes an F_q̃-constraint when you sample a random a ∈ F_q̃ and evaluate. Each step costs a Schwartz-Zippel-style soundness factor, but each factor is negligible when q̃ is large enough.

### IOPPs over Q and the norm-growth problem

A hash-based SNARK commits to large vectors by encoding them with a linear error-correcting code and Merkle-hashing the codeword. The IOPP (IOP of Proximity) lets the verifier check, with a few queries, that what's hashed is close to a valid codeword. Brakedown and Ligero are the textbook constructions. Over a finite field F_q this is mechanical: encode with a Reed-Solomon code via FFT, take random linear combinations, query a few positions, done.

Over Q, two things go wrong. First, "random linear combination" is no longer a fixed-size operation: if a message has B-bit coefficients and you sum N of them, the result has B + log N bits, not B. Second, and worse, Reed-Solomon encoding over Q means evaluating polynomials over a fixed set S ⊂ Z, which produces codeword entries of size roughly ||x||_∞ · ||S||^{k−1}_∞. For practical code dimensions k around 2¹⁰, you get codewords with millions of bits per entry. The naive lift of Reed-Solomon to Q is unusable.

The paper's IPRS code is the workaround: lift the FFT *algorithm* rather than the polynomial-evaluation *definition*, and prove the result is a valid Q-code with the right distance properties.

## The construction

Zinc+ has two layers stacked on top of each other. The **outer layer** is a polynomial IOP (PIOP) compiler that turns any finite-field PIOP — Hyperplonk-style, Binius-style, anything sumcheck-based — into a PIOP for the UCS relation. The **inner layer** is Zip+, the hash-based IOPP that handles the polynomial commitments over Q[X]. The compiler produces an IOP, and a standard BCS / Fiat-Shamir transform produces the final non-interactive SNARK.

### The three-step reduction

The compiler's main move is reducing constraints over R[X] to constraints over a finite field. Each PIOP round does this in three steps; each step has its own soundness contribution.

```rust
/// One round of Zinc+'s PIOR reduction.
/// Input:  a UCS constraint Q(b, x, w) ∈ (g) over R[X] for all b ∈ {0,1}^μ
///         (R is one of Q, F_q for prime-power q).
/// Output: an equivalent equality over F_{q̃} that a standard finite-field PIOP can verify.
fn zinc_plus_round(
    constraint: IdealConstraint<R>,         // Q(b, y, f) ∈ (g) over R[X]
    instance: &PublicInputs,
    witness_oracle: PolyOracle<R>,          // [[f̃]], evaluable under projections
    transcript: &mut Transcript,
) -> EqualityConstraint<Fqt> {

    // Step 1: project R[X] → F_{q̃}[X].
    //   If R = Q:    sample a random Ω(λ)-bit prime q0 and apply φ_{q0} coefficient-wise.
    //                If q0 divides any explicit denominator, verifier rejects.
    //   If R = F_q:  embed into a large extension F_{q̃} via ι_{q̃} (identity if q already large).
    let qt: ExtensionOrPrime = sample_projection_target::<R>(transcript);
    let constraint_qt = project_ring(constraint, qt);     // now over F_{qt}[X]

    // Step 2: batch the 2^μ row-wise ideal-membership checks into one MLE evaluation claim.
    //   Verifier samples r ∈ F_{q̃}^μ; prover sends e ∈ F_{q̃}[X], supposedly equal to
    //       e = Σ_b Q(b, y, f) · ẽq(b, r).
    //   Verifier checks e ∈ (g̃). Membership of e implies row-wise membership w.h.p.
    let r: Vec<Fqt> = transcript.sample_vec(constraint_qt.mu);
    let e: PolyFqt = prover_send_mle_at_r(&constraint_qt, &r);
    transcript.observe(&e);
    verifier_check_ideal_membership(&e, &constraint_qt.generator);

    // The constraint is now the strict equality
    //   Σ_b Q(b, Y, Z) · ẽq(b, r) − e  =  0     over F_{q̃}[X].
    let eq_constraint: EqualityConstraintFqtX =
        constraint_qt.fold_to_equality(&r, &e);

    // Step 3: evaluation projection F_{q̃}[X] → F_{q̃}.
    //   Verifier samples a ∈ F_{q̃} (a primitive element) and evaluates the polynomial
    //   identity at X = a. Witness oracles are queried under ψ_a from now on.
    let a: Fqt = transcript.sample();
    eq_constraint.evaluate_at(a)    // plain F_{q̃}-equality, ready for an ordinary PIOP
}
```

After these three steps every R[X]-constraint has become a vanilla F_q̃ algebraic constraint, modulo one twist: the polynomial oracles are now **projected** oracles. The prover committed to f̃ over R[X], but the verifier queries ψ_a(f̃) at points in F_q̃. Concretely, when R = Q the oracle's contract is: return ψ_a(f̃)(β) when the projection is well-defined at that point, return ⊥ otherwise (which the verifier treats as a rejection). The IOPP — Zip+ — has to enforce that ⊥ is rare for honest provers and that cheating projections get caught.

### IPRS codes

Reed-Solomon over Q in the obvious sense — evaluate a degree-<k polynomial at integer points — fails because codeword bit-size blows up exponentially in k. The paper's fix is to lift the **encoder** rather than the code definition. Take a radix-2 FFT algorithm for RS over a small prime field F_q, replace every twiddle factor ω^j with its centered integer representative ι(ω^j) ∈ {−(q−1)/2, …, (q−1)/2}, replace every base-case Vandermonde entry similarly, and run the same recursive algorithm over Q without ever reducing modulo q. The output is what the paper calls Integer Pseudo-Reed-Solomon (IPRS).

```rust
/// IPRS radix-2 encoder. Same algorithmic shape as the RS FFT, but every twiddle
/// factor is lifted to a centered integer in Z and arithmetic stays over Q.
///
/// Invariant: at every level, `out[j] = f0(ω^{2j}) + ω^j · f1(ω^{2j})` evaluated
/// over Q with the lifted twiddles. The result is NOT a polynomial evaluation;
/// it's the image of an integer linear map whose minimum-distance and norm
/// properties are proved by viewing IPRS as a non-Vandermonde lift of the
/// underlying F_q RS code.
fn iprs_encode_radix2(
    x: &[Rational],            // message of length k = 2^t
    twiddles_lifted: &[i64],   // ι(ω^j) for j ∈ [0, n)
    depth: usize,              // recursion depth before base case
) -> Vec<Rational> {
    if x.len() <= base_case_threshold() {
        // Base case: multiply by the lifted Vandermonde matrix directly over Q.
        return naive_lifted_vandermonde(x, twiddles_lifted);
    }
    let (even, odd): (Vec<_>, Vec<_>) = split_even_odd(x);
    let f0 = iprs_encode_radix2(&even, twiddles_lifted, depth - 1);
    let f1 = iprs_encode_radix2(&odd,  twiddles_lifted, depth - 1);
    // Butterfly combine over Q — no modular reduction at any layer.
    butterfly_combine_over_q(&f0, &f1, twiddles_lifted)
}
```

What you get is a Q-linear code of dimension k and length n with **MDS** relative distance δ = 1 − k/n + 1/n — the same as a real Reed-Solomon code — and codeword norm growth bounded by ||x||_∞ · (q/2)^{depth+1} · k. The paper's target configurations use q ≈ 2¹⁶, depth 1–2, radix 8, and k ≈ 2⁹ to 2¹⁰, yielding 40–60 bits of relative norm growth. For comparison, a naive RS lift over Q at the same dimension would produce codewords with thousands or millions of bits per entry.

> The subtle bit: IPRS codes are **not** Reed-Solomon codes lifted, and their codewords are not vectors of polynomial evaluations. The proof of the distance and norm bounds works by showing that IPRS is a *different* lift (Lemma 2.12 in the paper) of the underlying F_q RS code, using a non-Vandermonde generator matrix obtained implicitly from the FFT structure. That step preserves dimension and the F_q minimum distance, and the norm-growth analysis is then a separate accounting over Q. Whether IPRS has Mutual Correlated Agreement up to the Johnson bound — which would directly shrink Zinc+'s proof size — is an open question the paper flags; they fall back to the unique-decoding radius and the 1.5 Johnson bound for the soundness analysis.

### Zip+: Brakedown over Q with bit-size accounting

Zip+ is the IOPP that proves "I committed to a multilinear polynomial f̃ with coefficients in Q^{<d,B}[X], and ψ(f̃)(z) = ζ in some finite field F_m". The skeleton is Brakedown: arrange the witness as a k₂ × k₁ matrix W, encode each row with IPRS to get an interleaved codeword V ∈ Q^{k₂ × n}, Merkle-commit V column-by-column.

```rust
/// Zip+ prover for a projected MLE evaluation claim.
///   ψ : Z_{(p)}[X] → F_m  is the canonical projection (mod p, then evaluate at α).
///   Claim: ψ(W̃)(z) = ζ  in F_m, with z ∈ F_m^μ and ζ ∈ F_m.
///
/// Three things differ from a finite-field Brakedown:
///   1. The random linear combination is over Q, not over a small field; the prover
///      sends an actual rational vector whose bit-size is bounded-checked.
///   2. The check has a built-in gap: honest provers use bit-size ≤ B0, soundness
///      only rejects above B = poly(B0). The interval (B0, B) is tolerated, with
///      the gap budgeted into the security parameter.
///   3. When ψ goes through a large extension F_{p^κ}, only the tensor vectors
///      u1, u2 are lifted (degree ≤ κ), not the evaluation point z (which would
///      blow up to degree κμ in X).
fn zip_plus_prove(
    cfg: &Zip,
    W:   &Matrix<RatPoly>,          // witness, k2 × k1 entries in Q^{<d,B}[X]
    V:   &Matrix<RatPoly>,          // interleaved IPRS encoding of W's rows
    z:   &[Fm],
    zeta: Fm,
    transcript: &mut Transcript,
) {
    let (z2, z1) = split(z);                        // z = (z_2, z_1)
    let u2 = eq_vector(z2);                         // ∈ F_m^{k2}
    let u1 = eq_vector(z1);                         // ∈ F_m^{k1}

    // (a) Prover sends a := u2^T · φ_m(W) · u1, a polynomial in F_m[X].
    //     Verifier checks ψ(a) = ζ.
    let a: PolyFm = mul_tensor(&u2, &phi_m(W), &u1);
    transcript.observe(&a);
    assert_eq!(project_eval(&a, z), zeta);

    // (b) Reduce the projected claim to a non-projected tensor claim by sampling
    //     a fresh large prime m' and a fresh ξ ∈ F_{m'}, then checking
    //         ψ_{m',ξ}(u2_lift)^T · φ_{m'}(W) · ψ_{m',ξ}(u1_lift) = ψ_{m',ξ}(a).
    let m_fresh = transcript.sample_prime();
    let xi = transcript.sample::<Fm>();
    let (u2_p, u1_p, a_p) = project_for_proximity(&u2, &u1, &a, m_fresh, xi);

    // (c) Final step: prover commits to a random Q-linear combination of W's rows
    //     (the underlying *message* w_star, not the encoded codeword). Verifier:
    //       - Bit-size check: |w_star_i| ≤ B0 * POLY_SLACK for all i.
    //       - Tensor-constraint check holds on w_star modulo m_fresh.
    //       - Merkle openings of V at queried column indices equal IPRS-Enc(w_star)
    //         at those positions, over Q.
    let challenge: Vec<Fm> = transcript.sample_vec(cfg.k2);
    let w_star: Vec<Rational> = linear_combination_over_q(&W.rows(), &challenge);
    enforce_bit_size(&w_star, cfg.bit_size_bound * POLY_SLACK);
    open_columns_and_check(V, &w_star, transcript);
}
```

Three things to flag about Zip+ versus a finite-field Brakedown. First, the random linear combination is taken over Q, so the prover sends an actual rational vector whose bit-size is checked — there's no "everything fits in 64 bits" shortcut. Second, the bit-size check has a built-in gap: honest provers use witnesses of bit-size at most B₀, while soundness only guarantees rejection above some B > B₀; the interval in between is tolerated and budgeted into the security parameter. Third, when the projection ψ goes through a large extension field F_{p^κ} (which happens for F₂-based arithmetizations of SHA-256), naively lifting the evaluation point to Q[X] blows up the X-degree to κμ; Zip+ avoids this by lifting only the tensor vectors u₁ and u₂, which keeps the lifted degree at d + 2κ − 2.

## Why it's secure

The high-level reduction chain is, in order: UCS-over-R[X] → ideal-membership-over-F_q̃[X] → equality-over-F_q̃[X] → equality-over-F_q̃ → the base PIOP's relation. Each arrow loses some soundness, and each loss is independently negligible.

The **ring projection** step is the only one specific to Zinc+. If a Q[X] polynomial Q(b, y, f) is not in the ideal (g), it has a nonzero remainder r after Euclidean division by g. Reducing r modulo a random Ω(λ)-bit prime q₀ kills it only if q₀ divides every coefficient of r, which happens for at most polynomially many primes; in λ bits of prime entropy, that's negligible. The **ideal batching** step uses a generalization of the standard Schwartz-Zippel argument: if any entry of a vector v ∈ F_q̃[X]^n is outside the ideal (g), then v̂(r) ∈ (g) for a random r ∈ F_q̃^μ holds with probability at most μ/|F_q̃|. The proof projects onto the quotient F_q̃[X]/(g) and applies Schwartz-Zippel inside the quotient. The **evaluation projection** is just Schwartz-Zippel again: a degree-(d · d_Q) polynomial vanishes at a random a ∈ F_q̃ with probability at most d · d_Q / |F_q̃|. And the base PIOP's round-by-round knowledge soundness carries through unchanged, except in the one round where the new uniformly random query is introduced — there it picks up an extra ν/|F_q̃| term (Theorem 5.11).

The Zip+ IOPP is the more interesting half. Its soundness proof relies on a property called **Mutual Correlated Agreement** (MCA): roughly, that if a random F-linear combination of vectors is close to the code, then those vectors were jointly close to codewords agreeing in the same set of positions. Over finite fields, MCA up to the 1.5 Johnson bound is known from [Zei24]. The paper adapts that proof to linear codes over Q, which is one of the side contributions and the reason the IOPP can work in the list-decoding regime instead of being stuck at the unique-decoding radius.

> The genuinely subtle part of Zip+'s soundness is the **bit-size gap**. The IOPP is designed to reject witnesses whose entries are larger than the honest bound, but the random linear combinations the soundness proof leans on don't preserve bit-sizes exactly — summing N rationals each of bit-size B gives something around B + log N bits, with some probability of being smaller. The proof accepts a gap: honest provers stay below B₀, sound rejection only kicks in above some B = poly(B₀), and adversarial provers who land between are tolerated because the cost is bounded by the security parameter. If you're auditing an implementation, the constants in this gap and how they propagate through the IOPP rounds are where most of the parameter-tuning subtlety lives — and where a buggy implementation will silently lose soundness.

## Performance

Benchmarked on a MacBook Air M4 (16 GB), multi-threaded, at 100 bits of security, proving 7 consecutive SHA-256 compressions followed by the multi-scalar-multiplication part of ECDSA verification on secp256k1:

| System | Prover | Verifier | Proof | Setup | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Zinc+** (this work) | 40.6 ms | 7.0 ms | 198 KB | transparent | 19 trace columns × 29 rows, unoptimized, M4 |
| Groth16 (typical) | seconds | ~1 ms | ~200 B | trusted, pairing curve | full ECDSA expensive, requires non-native arithmetic gadgets |
| Plonkish over F_q (e.g. Halo2-style on SHA-256) | hundreds of ms | ms | 50–500 KB | transparent | thousands of trace columns for the SHA-256 part alone |
| Binius on bit-heavy circuits | tens of ms | ms | hundreds of KB | transparent | F₂^k native; AND, rotation, mod-2^w still emulated |

The 198 KB proof is not impressive in isolation — Groth16 fits in a tweet — but it lands inside the range of transparent hash-based SNARKs, on a circuit whose total witness column count is 19. That second number is the one to look at. Typical SHA-256 arithmetizations over F_q use several hundred to several thousand witness columns; Zinc+ pulls the same statement into 29 rows × 19 columns by making rotations and XORs native operations rather than constraint subcircuits. The reduction comes from the arithmetization framework, not from the underlying IOPP, so any future Zinc+ instantiation built on a smaller-proof backend (the paper signals WHIR is in progress) keeps the column savings.

Prover time decomposes mostly into the Zip+ IOPP commit (8.4 ms) and open (4.7 ms), the finite-field PIOP at 21.4 ms, and the three-step Zinc+ reduction itself at 5.5 ms. The proof compression line — 0.6 ms with zstd-3 cutting the size roughly in half — is a reminder that the wire format is rational-coefficient-heavy and benefits from generic compression in a way most SNARK proof formats don't. Verifier cost is dominated by the IOPP open at 4.2 ms and the PIOP at 2.0 ms.

The crossover point against finite-field competitors lives at the small-to-medium statement size. Zinc+'s message lengths in the benchmark are deliberately small (k around 2⁹ to 2¹⁰): the framework is built around natural arithmetizations, which fit in much fewer columns than zkVM-style encodings of the same computation. At very large circuit sizes IPRS encoding times start to dominate — Table 2 in the paper reports 8 ms for k = 2¹⁴, 32 ms for k = 2¹⁶, and 177 ms for k = 2¹⁸ when encoding degree-<32 binary-coefficient polynomials — and the Brakedown-style proof size grows as √n. A WHIR-style IOPP would cut proof size considerably without disturbing the rest of the stack; the authors flag this as ongoing work.

## Implementation notes

**Reference crate.** The reference implementation lives at `github.com/NethermindEth/zinc-plus`, currently on the `main-beta` branch where the published benchmarks come from. The `main` branch lags behind because, per the paper's acknowledgements, parts of the implementation were AI-assisted and are still being audited and integrated. If you're evaluating Zinc+ for production, treat the current code as a serious proof of concept rather than a hardened library.

**No drop-in alternative.** Zinc was the precursor (also from Nethermind and collaborators) and shares parts of the lineage. The other "SNARKs over rings" work — Zartan [CH24] and the concurrent Qāren [SSPP26] — uses hidden-order-group or generic-PCS approaches that don't share much code with hash-based stacks. If you want to build on Zinc+ today, you're forking the reference implementation; there's no second-source crate.

**Constant-time concerns.** Provers in hash-based SNARKs generally don't carry a constant-time obligation: their inputs are the public statement plus their own secret witness, and the resulting proof is non-secret. The verifier is similarly fine. Where you'd care is if Zinc+ ever gets used inside a zero-knowledge wrapper — the current scheme is not ZK — and the witness contains long-term secrets. Rational-coefficient arithmetic with variable bit-size is not constant-time by default, so any future ZK version will need to either pad bit-sizes or move witness handling into a constant-time bignum layer. The verifier's hash and Merkle operations are constant-time by structure as long as the underlying hash is.

**Serialization and domain separation.** The witness is rational-coefficient-heavy, and there's no canonical serialization for an arbitrary rational. The reference implementation picks a canonical form — reduced numerator/denominator pair — and serializes that, with the bit-size bound enforced lexically before any cryptographic check. Domain separation for the Fiat-Shamir transcript needs to commit to the random prime q₀, the extension field choice F_q̃, and the IPRS code parameters (base field, radix, depth); reusing any one of these across instances without rebinding gives an attacker free entropy.

**Side-channel concerns at the prover.** The most exotic prover step is sampling a random Ω(λ)-bit prime q₀ *after* observing the witness, then projecting the witness modulo q₀. If your prime sampler has visible timing variance depending on which prime gets picked, that's leaking witness information through q₀'s choice. Use a constant-time primality test, or sample from a precomputed pool — neither is something a typical Rust crypto crate ships with for bignum primes.

**Gotchas particular to ideal-membership constraints.** Three things to watch. First, the lookup constraints that enforce typing — a witness element belongs to {0,1}^{<32}[X], or to Z — are not optional decoration. Every example in Section 2.1.3 of the paper relies on the typing being enforced; skipping a lookup converts a soundness proof into a comment. Second, when you write a modular-squaring constraint v̂ − û² + n · μ ∈ (X − 2), the extra witness variable μ has its own range constraint that must be small; large μ silently breaks the equivalence. Third, the bit-size bound on rational witnesses is a circuit-defined parameter; changing it requires re-running the soundness accounting, not just bumping a number.

## Limitations

The current Zinc+ scheme is not zero-knowledge. Adding ZK is on the authors' roadmap, but the bit-size accounting in Zip+ interacts with hiding in non-obvious ways — you can't just blind a rational coefficient without also extending the bit-size budget, and the witness shape leaks information through which projections are well-defined. If your application needs ZK today, the published scheme is not yet what you want.

Proof size is the other obvious cost: 198 KB at 100-bit security on a 19-column circuit is two to three orders of magnitude larger than a Groth16 proof of comparable statements. Brakedown-style IOPPs have √n proof sizes by construction, and the authors are explicit that switching to WHIR or a similar IOPP should cut this considerably — but until that work lands, Zinc+ is for settings that already accept hundred-kilobyte proofs.

The ECDSA story in the benchmark is partial. The 40.6 ms / 198 KB number proves the multi-scalar-multiplication step, which is the dominant cost, but the three mod-n operations that complete signature verification (where n is secp256k1's scalar prime) aren't yet in the reference code — they require constraints over multiple rings simultaneously, and the current implementation only handles a single Z[X] arithmetization. The framework supports them; the code doesn't yet.

The strongest assumption underneath is the random oracle model for the hash used in Fiat-Shamir and Merkle commitments. Beyond that, Zinc+ relies on the collision resistance of that hash and on the MCA property of IPRS codes up to the 1.5 Johnson bound — the latter being an adaptation of [Zei24] to Q that the paper proves directly. Whether IPRS codes have MCA up to the full Johnson bound, which would tighten parameters and shrink proofs, is open.

The crossover against zkVM-style approaches is where the benefit and the cost meet most sharply. If you're proving a small, structured computation — a few hashes plus a signature, a TLS handshake transcript, an authenticated data structure — Zinc+'s arithmetization-overhead savings dominate and you win on prover time and circuit size. If you're proving execution of a large arbitrary program, the small-message-length regime where IPRS encoding is fastest stops being relevant, and the zkVM advantage of "one giant uniform circuit, reuse the same proving infrastructure" pulls ahead. Reach for Zinc+ when you control the relation, the relation is bit-heavy or modular-arithmetic-heavy, and 200 KB of proof at 100-bit security is a price you'd happily pay to avoid hand-arithmetizing SHA-256 into a finite field one more time.
